# Trading Bias Part 1

## Portal Summary & Highlights

This session explores the concept of trading bias and how to determine market direction using price bar analysis, previous day highs and lows, and the fractal method. We learn how to combine the four components of a price bar (open, high, low, close) with key levels, market structure shifts, and liquidity concepts to identify high-probability trade setups while managing risk effectively.

### Highlights

- [00:00] Definition of trading bias: a predisposition toward a higher probability outcome for an asset. The key question is whether price is more likely to trade bullish or bearish, which is answered by identifying where price is trading away from Ñ a key level, swing high, swing low, liquidity pool, gap, or broken market structure.
- [01:11] Determining bias through bar components: understanding the four components of a price bar (open, high, low, close) is essential. Each bar's open allows decisions based on the prior bar's close, while past highs and lows become targets for future plans.
- [02:19] Using fractals to maintain bias: select two swing lows or swing highs back on the chosen timeframe. Only switch bias when that key swing level is broken Ñ until then, continue trading with the trend.
- [03:07] Reading individual bars:
  - Close above open = green/bullish bar
  - Close below open = red/bearish bar
  - Large distance between open and close = significant volatility and conviction
  - Close near open = indecision or lack of conviction
  - Open far from previous close = a gap, representing unfinished business
- [04:38] Example of a weak bar: an asset that sells off and closes near its session low indicates sellers in control late in the session. When the following bar opens and continues lower without trading higher, it confirms bearish continuation.
- [06:10] Core rule using daily highs and lows: never long above a previous day high; never short below a previous day low. These levels are where profits are taken with limit orders and where stop losses are placed, since no one knows with certainty whether price will continue or reverse at these points.
- [07:48] Liquidity grab vs trend shift: if a bullish asset retraces to a previous day low but spends little time there and closes back inside (ideally on a 15-minute chart), it should be read as bullish liquidity collection rather than a bearish shift.
- [08:53] Bearish trend example on Bitcoin: consecutive lower daily lows being broken with no previous day highs taken confirms a bearish bias. In such conditions, we look for shorts and avoid longs beyond quick scalps.
- [11:30] Recognizing a fakeout: a quick liquidity grab above a prior high followed by an immediate move back inside, especially when confirmed with order flow (open interest decreasing on the up candle), signals the move is being faded and shorts remain in control.
- [13:17] Bullish trend example: eight consecutive days of broken previous day highs with no previous day lows taken confirms strong bullish bias. We take profits above each previous day high, re-enter on retracements, and remain long.
- [16:49] First sign of weakness: the first time a previous day low is broken within a long bullish sequence signals a potential shift. We do not short immediately Ñ instead, we wait for a retracement to at least the 50% Fibonacci level, re-anchoring the fib on each new pivot low until confirmed.
- [19:00] Switching gears to shorts: once trend shift is confirmed, risk flips. Shorts are entered on retracements with stops just above the recent swing high, targeting areas with two previous day lows clustered together for liquidity.
- [22:12] Williams Fractals explained:
  - Bullish fractal: a low with two higher-low bars on each side (marked by a down arrow)
  - Bearish fractal: a high with two lower-high bars on each side (marked by an up arrow)
  - Entry occurs at the open of the third bar after the arrow forms
- [25:00] Fractal application at a key level (Oil example): price reaches a prior swing high, forms multiple bullish fractals, flirts with the level, and impulsively clears a bearish fractal. The combination of key level + fractal break + market structure shift creates a high-probability long setup with small risk and high reward.
- [28:00] Entering on retracement: rather than longing at the breakout high, draw a fib from low to high and enter between the 0.618Ð0.66 CC zone, placing stops just below for optimal risk-to-reward.
- [32:00] Weekly bar analysis: evaluating weekly candles by their open/close relationship reveals bias. Candles with closes far from the open show conviction, while candles closing near the open indicate indecision. The majority of up-closing candles in a sequence confirms sustained bullish bias.
- [37:30] Swing trading with fractals on the weekly: so long as each new bullish fractal low holds and prior highs continue to break, the long position is maintained. The first broken fractal low signals weakness, but with stops placed two higher-lows back, the position can survive early shakeouts.
- [41:00] Confirmed trend reversal: when a single candle breaks multiple prior weekly swing lows, the trend is confirmed as shifted. We sell accumulated longs and look for shorts on retracement into the breakdown level (often aligning with the CC zone).
- [44:00] Key takeaways:
  - Master the four bar components to read price action
  - Stick with trend direction while majority of bars close in that direction (60Ð70%)
  - Train the eye to recognize swing highs/lows and fractals forming at key levels
  - Use previous day high/low to frame daily bias and profit targets
  - Combine with order flow tools (CVD, open interest, volume) for confirmation
  - Apply the "if there, then below; if below, then above" liquidity principle on both long and short setups

We are encouraged to review these concepts carefully, backtest them across multiple assets and timeframes, and apply them with disciplined risk management to build consistent trading decisions.
