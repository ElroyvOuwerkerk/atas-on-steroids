# Fibonacci (session 05)

## Portal Summary & Highlights

This session explores how trading bots may operate using Fibonacci retracement and extension levels on lower market cap altcoins. We learn how to identify algorithmic footprints by observing repeated reactions at specific Fibonacci levels, how to anticipate breakouts based on the number of touches, and how to combine this observation with traditional support, resistance, and confluence factors for trade planning.

### Highlights

- [00:00] Introduction to the concept that many bot algorithms operate around Fibonacci levels, since Fibs allow programmable entries, exits, and stop losses. Example: a bot programmed to buy at the 0.5 retracement and sell near the negative 0.618 extension, with a hard stop loss below the 0.786 level.
- [00:47] Why this approach works better on lower market cap coins:
  - Higher cap assets like Ethereum have more human participation, which can obscure the algorithmic footprint.
  - Lower cap coins often produce cleaner, more precise Fibonacci reactions.
- [01:15] Key Fibonacci levels to map out, including both negative retracement levels (0, -0.5, -1, -1.5, -2, -2.5, -3, etc.) and positive expansion levels (1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3) to track the full algorithmic range.
- [01:54] Practical concept: by identifying the algo working on a coin, we can align our trades with the bot’s programmed behavior, especially during periods dominated by automated activity.
- [02:19] Case study on WAVES:
  - Pivot drawn from high to low; the critical confirmation comes from price reacting at the 0.5 retracement.
  - Reaction should stay contained around 0.5 — not push through to the 0.618 or 0.786.
  - Further confirmation comes from clean reactions at negative extensions (-1, -1.5, -2).
- [04:22] Touch count rule for anticipating breaks:
  - First touch: bounce expected.
  - Second touch: bounce often still holds.
  - Third touch: high probability of break-through, as the bot’s buying or selling liquidity at that level becomes depleted.
- [05:13] Stop-loss clustering insight: human traders place stops below repeated lows. When price breaks those lows, the cascade of triggered stops can cause sharp extensions past the next Fibonacci level (e.g., skipping -1.5 and reaching -2).
- [06:00] Forward-looking analysis exercise: after multiple touches of a level, anticipate continuation of the algo pattern. The example shows price breaking through the -2 level cleanly to reach -2.5, aligning with bullish divergences and oversold conditions — a confluence where human participants may also see value.
- [07:22] Case study on BAT (Basic Attention Token):
  - Repeated precise touches of the 0.5 level "to the exact sat" suggesting strong algorithmic presence.
  - Sequence of clean reactions across multiple Fibonacci levels (0.5, -1, -1.5, -2, -2.5).
  - Reinforces the touch-count principle — many touches eventually lead to a clean break.
- [09:24] Case study on LINK: precise reactions on the 0.5 and negative 0.5 levels confirm algorithmic structure, then transitions into a real trade example.
- [10:19] Building a trade with confluence:
  - Monthly level support.
  - 0.382 retracement.
  - Negative 0.618 extension.
  - Algorithmic Fibonacci footprint.
  - Multiple overlapping factors strengthen the case for a long entry.
- [10:55] Best practice reminder: continue using standard Fibonacci tools on daily, weekly, and monthly timeframes. The algo-Fib method is an additional layer of analysis, not a replacement for higher-timeframe structure.

We encourage viewers to study these examples carefully, test the touch-count and Fibonacci-confluence concepts on their own charts, and always apply disciplined risk management before acting on any observed pattern.
