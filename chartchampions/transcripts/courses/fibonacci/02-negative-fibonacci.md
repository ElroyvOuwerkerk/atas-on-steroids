# Negative Fibonacci

## Portal Summary & Highlights

This session explores the use of negative Fibonacci levels as a confluence tool for identifying potential targets and entries after price moves beyond a standard Fibonacci retracement range. We review the key negative levels used, the distinction between internal and external retracements, and practical chart examples demonstrating how negative Fibonacci levels can mark areas of support, resistance, and take-profit zones when combined with other technical tools.

### Highlights

- [00:00] Introduction to negative Fibonacci levels as a confluence tool rather than a standalone strategy. The emphasis is on stacking multiple reasons before taking a trade.
- [00:44] Key negative Fibonacci levels to add to the charting tool:
  - Negative 0.236
  - Negative 0.618
  - Negative 1
  - Negative 1.618
- [01:11] On stock markets, the negative 0.272 is often preferred. Comparing the 0.272 with the 0.236 helps identify which level has stronger confluence. The 0.382 can also occasionally be referenced, though adding too many close levels reduces clarity.
- [02:16] Primary use of negative levels:
  - Targets while in a position
  - Potential support or resistance zones once price moves externally from the original retracement range
  - Entries and exits as part of a leading-indicator approach
- [02:54] Walkthrough of an uptrend example: drawing a Fib from swing low to swing high, identifying support near the 0.618 level, and confirming the entry with additional confluence such as old resistance flipping to support.
- [03:53] Risk reminder: buying every 0.618 without added confluence is not a reliable approach. At least two or three layers of confluence are recommended before entering.
- [04:19] Example of a scaled entry structure: splitting orders between the 0.618, mid-zone of the 0.618 to 0.66, and the 0.66 level to improve average entry.
- [04:39] Using negative Fibs for take-profit zones: Horizontals remain the primary target tool, with Fib levels, extensions, and harmonics adding further confluence.
  - TP1 at the negative 0.236
  - TP2 at the negative 0.618
  - Final target at the negative 1 (a 1:1 extension of the prior move)
- [05:50] Litecoin example: Fib drawn from high to low. Price repeatedly tested the 0.5 before finding support at the negative 0.236, demonstrating how the level can act as a reaction zone for either a target or a potential long entry.
- [06:57] Key principle: Negative Fibonacci levels should always be combined with another technical tool to validate the entry or target.
- [07:09] Weak-market behavior: price may top out at the 0.382 without reaching the 0.5, forming a lower high and then rolling over to find support at the negative 0.236.
- [07:48] Internal vs. external retracements:
  - Internal: price action remaining within the swing high and swing low of the current Fib range.
  - External: price breaks beyond the original range, requiring a different Fib technique such as extensions for projecting future levels.
- [09:45] Bitcoin example: Fib from low to high showed price returning to the 0.618 ("Champions Channel"), with confluence from a monthly level, followed by a rejection at the negative 0.236 and continuation to the negative 0.618.
- [11:00] Parallel channel confluence example: On a different chart, the negative 0.236 formed the bottom of a parallel channel that supported price for an extended period, illustrating how negative Fib levels can define the structure of trending channels.
- [12:05] Summary of practical uses: identifying bounces, projecting targets, defining channel boundaries, and confirming reaction zones Ñ always alongside complementary technical confluence.

We encourage reviewing these Fibonacci concepts carefully and practicing their application on historical charts, ensuring that every entry and target is supported by multiple layers of confluence and sound risk management.
