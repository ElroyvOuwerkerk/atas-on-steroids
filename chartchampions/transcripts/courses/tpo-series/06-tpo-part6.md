# TPO Part 6

## Portal Summary & Highlights

This session explains two TPO-based day trading concepts: ledges as intraday support and resistance levels, and the double distribution (DD) setup for trading between two profile distributions separated by a row of single prints. We learn how to identify these structures, when to look for swing failure patterns, when to expect acceptance and continuation, and—critically—when to avoid counter-trend trades against a developing DD profile.

### Highlights

- [00:00] Introduction to ledges and double distribution setups as day trading tools derived from the TPO chart.
- [00:06] Definition of a ledge: two or more TPO blocks stacked at the same price in the middle of the profile, similar in structure to a poor high or poor low but located inside the distribution.
- [00:51] Ledges function as intraday support and resistance levels; primarily relevant for day traders rather than swing traders.
- [01:22] Visual breakdown of how a ledge appears on the profile and how a ledge resistance can flip into support once broken.
- [01:46] Personal rule of thumb: a ledge with four or more TPO blocks that initially rejects price is more likely to be broken on a subsequent retest — always context dependent.
- [02:25] Confirmation tools to monitor on retest:
  - CVD
  - Open interest
  - Delta
  - Volume
- [02:47] Why ledges matter: the area above/below a ledge represents liquidity, making it a candidate for swing failure pattern trades.
- [03:18] First-priority setup at a ledge is always the swing failure pattern; if no SFP forms and the level breaks, look for a long on a retest/backtest of the ledge.
- [03:50] Volume and order flow signature for a valid breakout-retest:
  - Breakout: rising open interest, rising volume, positive delta
  - Retest: declining open interest, declining volume, neutral delta
- [04:42] Introduction to the double distribution setup: distribution ? quick move ? row of single prints ? second distribution.
- [05:04] Core rule: once price gains acceptance into the row of single prints, expect a move to the opposite end of the single print row.
- [05:19] Anatomy of a DD day walked through on a profile: initial balance, first distribution, rapid move leaving single prints, second distribution forming.
- [06:07] Identifying the key levels — the low and high of the single prints become the trade boundaries for the DD setup.
- [06:41] Two valid trade variants on a DD setup:
  - Swing failure pattern: a quick wick into the single prints that is rejected.
  - Acceptance trade: sustained acceptance into the row, targeting the opposite side of the single prints (gap fill).
- [07:27] Example showing acceptance into the single prints, target reached at the top of the row, followed by a clean backtest of the low of the single prints holding as support.
- [08:21] Directional discipline: never short into an upside acceptance of a DD setup, and never long into a downside acceptance — trade only in the direction of the gap fill.
- [08:38] Key takeaway: the DD framework is most valuable for knowing when NOT to trade — avoiding counter-trend entries even when other confluences appear.
- [09:23] Why DD setups produce short squeezes (or long squeezes): the rapid initial move builds no volume-based SR levels, so counter-trend traders get repeatedly stopped out.
- [10:50] Historical context: DD “Bart” patterns were extremely common in crypto during 2018–2019, formed by repeated distribution ? single prints ? distribution cycles.
- [12:15] Summary of intraday SR hierarchy:
  - Ledges = intraday SR levels
  - Single prints = SR levels (resistance until accepted, then support)
  - Acceptance into single prints = trigger for the DD setup
- [12:42] Order of execution preference: always look for a swing failure pattern at a ledge or single print boundary first, before considering an acceptance/continuation trade.

We can apply these concepts by carefully reading profile structure, prioritizing swing failure patterns, confirming with order flow data, and respecting directional bias on double distribution days — always combined with disciplined risk management.
